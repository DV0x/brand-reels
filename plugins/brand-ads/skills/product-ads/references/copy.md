# Copy: from the wants table to hooks

The hook is the words on the picture of a still Meta ad. It comes from the research, never from a formula: the
research picks the angle; a hook's shape only says that angle in different ways; three checks and one rule decide what
is shown.

## 1. Who is looking
A stranger scrolling the feed on a phone, not shopping for this, gives a still ad about a second. They stop for
something about themselves: a want, a worry, a moment they recognise. There is no word or character limit: a
headline, a buyer's review, a note or a comparison can each carry a strong hook. What must work at once is the first
line the eye reads. A second line may carry the proof.

## 2. The angle, picked once
An angle is one want from the wants table (research.md, section 8), the buyers who have it and how much they already
know, and the product's proof. Pick 2 to 4 angles that are truly different (different wants, or different buyers),
favouring the wants many buyers have and rivals already pay to sell on.

- **Start from the want, never from what is unique about the brand.** The brand's difference is the proof inside the
  angle: people act on wants they already have.
- **How much the buyers already know decides the kind of hook** (Eugene Schwartz's five stages of awareness):

  | The buyers... | The hook... |
  |---|---|
  | don't feel the problem yet | a moment they recognise; the product comes later |
  | feel the problem, but have no answer | the want or the worry, said the way they'd say it |
  | are comparing answers | how this differs from what let them down before |
  | know the product, but haven't bought | the doubt that stopped them, answered with proof |
  | are ready | the offer, said plainly (only a real one) |

  Write for the rows that the user's answer at stop 1 points to (who will see these ads). Without an answer, write for
  the first three rows: that is who a cold ad reaches.
- Write each angle in one line at the top of `HOOKS.md`: "<the want> for <who> (<what they know>); proof: <the fact>".

## 3. Write from buyers' words
- **Buyers' words are the raw material for every shape:** the standout lines, the moments, the questions and the
  numbers in `research.md`, and the words buyers use for things. A buyer's own line, shown as theirs, is one shape
  among nine (section 4), not the default.
- **A line shown as a quote** (in quote marks, with stars or a name) is a real review from the brand's own shop, word
  for word (SKILL.md, rules).
- **Words from Reddit, marketplaces or rivals' ads** can shape a hook; they are never shown as a customer's words.
- **In the brand's voice** (the voice card), but as a person would say it.

## 4. Versions: one angle, different shapes
Shapes are never there to find the idea (the research did that), only to say the same angle in different ways, so the
versions are not one line reworded and the ads they become don't look alike. Meta groups ads that look or feel alike as
variations of one creative, and shows them to the same people.

Write about 6 versions of each angle. Pick each version's shape from what the research found: a shape needs its
material, and a shape with none is skipped, never filled with something invented. That holds for the details too: a
place, a time, an object or a habit in a moment or a confession comes from the material, never from you.

| Shape | What it does | What the research must supply | Fits buyers who... |
|---|---|---|---|
| The want, named | says what they want or what hurts, the way they'd say it, or names who it is for | the want, in buyers' words | feel the problem |
| A moment | a small scene from their day that they would recognise | a real moment, from the reviews, Reddit or the web | don't feel it yet; feel the problem |
| A question, answered | the doubt that holds them back, and the fact that settles it, said honestly | a real buyer's question or doubt, and the brand's answer | are comparing; know the product |
| A customer's words | a real review, word for word, shown as the buyer's | a verified review from the brand's own shop | know the product |
| A number | a count, a dose, a rating or a result buyers care about | a number from the facts or the reviews that the rule allows | are comparing; know the product |
| A confession | someone admits something real, in a buyer's voice, never made to look like a review | a feeling or a habit many reviews share | any row: it sounds like a person |
| A contrast | this next to what they use or believe now, never naming a rival | what buyers tried before, and a difference the brand can prove | are comparing |
| How it fits their day | how it works, or how little it asks of them | the brand's own how-to-use facts | feel the problem; know the product |
| The offer | a real offer, tied to the want | a live offer | are ready |

After the checks (section 5), keep the best 3 or 4 versions of each angle, in at least 3 different shapes, with a
customer's words at most once. When fewer than 3 shapes pass, say the material was thin; never make up the difference
with more quotes.

## 5. The three checks
A separate step, once all the versions are written: read each hook as if someone else wrote it. Each check is yes or
no; "partly", "mostly" or "weak" means no. A hook with any no is rewritten and checked again, or dropped; `HOOKS.md`
says which, and why.

1. **Can you picture it?** It holds a number, a moment or a thing someone can see, and it is true enough to be proven
   wrong.
2. **Could only this brand say it?** Judge the words on the picture, wherever they came from. Put a rival's name and
   numbers in: if the line still works, it is a template. A real review is a template too when a rival's buyer could
   have written the same words. The fix: make it specific to this product (its proof, its buyers' moment, its numbers),
   in any shape.
3. **Does it sound like a person?** Read it out loud, as one friend to another: a text, a chat, a call. If it sounds
   like an ad, a brochure or a chatbot, rewrite it the way a buyer would say it.
   - Words people never say to a friend: unlock, elevate, transform, discover, game-changer, holy grail, flawless,
     age-defying, toxin-free, radiant.
   - Ad patterns: "Say goodbye to X", "Not just X. It's Y.", "X meets Y", "Tired of X? Meet Y.", "Stop X. Start Y.",
     three one-word sentences, a run of short fragments, a list read off a label, a question the ad answers itself,
     exclamation marks.
   - What talk sounds like: contractions, the buyers' everyday words for things, one small real detail from the
     research (a time, a place, a person), the buyers' own language or mix of languages (Hinglish in India) when they
     write that way, unless the brand says not to.

## 6. The rule: Meta, and the country's own
Not taste: a hook that breaks it gets the ad rejected or the brand in trouble.
- **Never point at the reader's own body, health or condition, in a question or a statement:** no "you" or "your"
  next to a body part, a health problem or how someone looks. Meta bans ads that say or imply something about a
  person's health. Talk about the product and what it does instead.
- **Never make the reader feel worse about how they look.** Meta bans statements that someone's appearance is
  inferior.
- **No result promised within a set time** ("in 30 days") unless the brand publishes it with its qualifier, and no
  health or results claims beyond the brand's own.
- **Every number, claim and offer, and how to take it, exactly as the brand publishes it** (the facts list): never
  narrowed, widened or made more specific (a time of day, a meal or a number of days the brand didn't name). A
  comparison must be provable, and never names a rival.
- **No line that tells people not to buy.**
- **The country's own rules for the category.** Each country where the ads run has its own rules for skincare and
  supplement ads (in India, the ASCI Code, and FSSAI for supplements). Find the ones that apply in the research, from
  the regulator's own pages, write them in `research.md` with their sources, and check every hook against them too.

## 7. What you show at stop 2
Write about 6 versions of each angle (2 to 4 angles), run the checks and the rule as a separate step, keep the 3 or 4
per angle that pass, in at least 3 shapes (section 4), then show the best 2 or 3 angles: those whose want the most
buyers have, and whose hooks would most stop a stranger. For each angle:
- **its versions that passed:** the words on the picture, and each one's shape;
- **the want it speaks to,** and who has it (how much they know);
- **why it should sell:** the count, the buyers' words, the rivals running it, and the bet in one line: "Buyers who
  ___ believe ___; this hook bets that ___ makes them stop";
- **the proof:** the fact, and its source.

Show nothing that failed. The rest stay in `HOOKS.md`; a send-back starts from there.

## 8. HOOKS.md layout
```
# <Brand> <Product>: hooks (<date>)
## Angles
1. <the want> for <who> (<what they know>); proof: <the fact>. Evidence: <count, rivals>.
## Hooks
| # | Angle | Shape | Words on the picture | Picture it? | Only this brand? | A person? | Rule | Kept, or why not |
## Shown at stop 2, and the user's verdict
## Round 2 (if sent back): what changed, and why
```

Sources (for maintainers): Harry Dry's three tests (Marketing Examples: "Can I visualise it? Can I falsify it? Can
nobody else say this?"); Motion's open hook-writing skill (github.com/motion-team/creative-strategy-skills, MIT): "Read
it out loud — if it sounds like an ad, rewrite it"; Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising (awareness); Meta's policies on
personal attributes and on health and wellness; the ASCI Code; Meta for Business, "Demystifying Creative
Diversification" (16 Dec 2025): ads that look or feel alike are grouped as one creative. Notes and links: the brand-ads
research of 2026-10-12.

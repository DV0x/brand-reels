# Copy: the topic, the hook and the script

## Who is watching
A stranger, scrolling. They have **zero interest** in the brand, the category or the topic, and **zero context**: they
don't know the product, the jargon or why any of it matters. They give the video about a second and a half. Nothing
on screen may assume they care, or know, anything.

## The topic
- **Start from what the audience has and does,** never from the brand's fact: their fridge, their morning routine, the
  gadget they gave up on, the money they spend, the thing they do every day without thinking. The brand's fact arrives
  later, as the answer.
- A good topic has four parts: a moment or habit strangers share, a consequence they didn't know, a fact from this brand
  that answers it, and a picture code can draw.
- The best ones come from what customers actually say (questions, complaints, habits), not from the About page.

## The hook: the first 3 seconds
Three things work together: the first frame, the on-screen line and the voice. The voice says the on-screen line's exact
words.

1. **The first frame is the viewer's own object or moment,** readable in one second: their fridge door opening, the
   gadget at the back of the cupboard, the bill. Not the product, not the brand, not a symbol that needs decoding.
2. **The line names that real thing, literally, and adds a consequence they didn't know** that they can picture.
   "Coffee in the fridge soaks up the smell of your food." It's their habit and a surprise they can see.
3. **The alone test:** a stranger reads only the line, with no voice and no picture. Do they know exactly what it's about,
   and that it's about them? If they could ask "what?" or "of what?", it fails.
4. **The zero-interest test:** would someone who doesn't care about this category stop here, and why? If the honest
   answer needs them to care about the brand's topic already ("because they want to know about roast dates"), it fails.
   A clear line they already agree with also fails: "Coffee at home never tastes like café coffee" is true and clear,
   and gives them no reason to stay.
5. **Name the real thing.** No stand-ins that could mean something else: "another spoon" read as sugar; "it", "this",
   "fresh coffee" and "waiting period" fail the same way.
6. **Clear beats clever beats short.** No wordplay, puns, teasers, metaphors or slang with a second meaning ("peaked",
   "flat"). Nothing that only makes sense after the payoff. Eight plain words beat five that need decoding.
7. **Never at the viewer's expense.** No shaming ("you're doing it wrong"), no scare words, nothing gross next to food or
   skin. A surprise about their habit is fine; mocking them for it isn't.
8. **Problem first.** Product-first lines ("This serum has a 10-day...") read as ads or as a delivery problem.

Write 8 to 10 hooks, cut them to the 2 or 3 that pass every test, and show only those.

**Lines that failed with this user, and why:**
- "Another spoon won't fix it": vague, the spoon could be sugar.
- "Your fresh coffee isn't ready yet": vague, sounds like a delivery delay.
- "TOO FRESH TO BREW": needs knowledge a stranger doesn't have.
- "Coffee at home never tastes like café coffee": clear, but nothing new, so no reason to stay.
- A story that opened on two cups with wavy "smell" lines: a symbol the viewer has to decode.

**Lines of the right kind:**
- "Coffee in the fridge soaks up the smell of your food." (their habit plus a consequence they can see)
- "You bought a moka pot and used it twice." (their own moment, said plainly)

## The script
- **Length comes from the words:** 25 to 45 s, about 65 to 110 words. Never pad.
- **The shape:**
  1. The hook (0 to 3 s).
  2. Why it happens: the mechanism, which the picture shows.
  3. What to do, or the turn.
  4. The brand's fact as the answer and the proof.
  5. A payoff, and one simple thing to do.
- **The brand appears only as the answer,** after the problem is clear, usually past the halfway mark.
- **Written to be heard:** short sentences, contractions, one idea per line, words customers use ("coffee powder").
- **In the brand's voice** (the voice card in `research.md`): a warm host and a clinical expert say the same fact differently.
- **Every claim has a source** in `research.md`. Keep their numbers exactly as they publish them. No health or results
  claims beyond theirs. If unsure, cut the claim.
- **On screen:**
  - 3 to 5 statements: big type built into the scene. The hook is usually one, the end line another.
  - Captions carry everything else, phrase by phrase.
  - Never show the same words twice: a line that is a statement has no caption (`"show": "statement"`).
- **Emphasis:** mark one word or short phrase in about one caption in three with `*asterisks*`.
- **Pronunciation:** use `"say"` for prices, numbers, units and brand names the voice may misread ("₹499" -> "four
  ninety-nine rupees").

## Self-check before showing the script
- [ ] The hook passes the alone test and the zero-interest test, and opens on the viewer's own thing.
- [ ] Every on-screen line passes the alone test.
- [ ] No stand-ins, wordplay, teasers or second meanings anywhere.
- [ ] Every claim has a source in `research.md`.
- [ ] 3 to 5 statements, and no words shown twice.
- [ ] The brand comes in as the answer, not the opener.
- [ ] It ends with one clear thing to do.
- [ ] Read aloud, it sounds like a person in the brand's voice, not a brochure.
